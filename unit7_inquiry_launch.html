Statement of Inquiry

Our identity as global citizens is defined by the relationships we have with the people, environments, and species we share the planet with — and the choices we make ripple outward to shape lives far beyond our own.

The Four Crises — Your Briefing Room Stations
Crisis 01 · Linked to Concept 1 — What Is Life?

Outbreak in Motion

01
🦠
🩺
Career Lens
Epidemiologist · Disease Ecologist
⚠️ Crisis Brief

It's 5:47 AM. Your phone buzzes. A new disease outbreak has been reported — three patients with severe respiratory symptoms in a remote province. Within 48 hours, cases could double. Within a week, this could cross borders.

You're the epidemiologist on call. Your job: figure out what's spreading, how it's spreading, who's at risk — and stop it before it becomes the next pandemic. But here's the puzzle: is the thing you're chasing even alive? Viruses don't fit neatly into the definition of "life" you'll learn this unit. That matters more than you'd think.

📍 DC Connection The CDC sits 600 miles from DC. The NIH is in Bethesda. Decisions made in our backyard shape outbreak responses worldwide — from Ebola in West Africa to COVID-19's global spread to seasonal flu surveillance. When DC sneezes, the world watches.
🎯 Triage Questions (jot quick notes at this station)
  1. What does an epidemiologist actually do day-to-day?
  2. Why is the question "Is a virus alive?" surprisingly important?
  3. Name one global outbreak from the last 25 years and where it started.

Crisis 02 · Linked to Concept 2 — The Water Cycle

The Water Crisis

02
💧
🚰
Career Lens
Water Resource Engineer · WASH Worker
⚠️ Crisis Brief

Right now, 2 billion people on Earth do not have safely managed drinking water. In some communities, children miss school to walk miles for water. In others, a single chemical spill upstream poisons an entire town downstream — sometimes thousands of miles away.

You're a water resource engineer or WASH (Water, Sanitation, Hygiene) worker. You design systems that bring clean water to communities and keep contamination out. But the same global water cycle that connects DC to Flint to Gaza to Bangladesh means your problems are everyone's problems. Water doesn't care about borders.

📍 DC Connection Rain that falls on DCI's roof flows down storm drains, into the Anacostia and Potomac rivers, into the Chesapeake Bay, into the Atlantic. A plastic bottle thrown out in our hallway can end up inside a sea turtle off the coast of Spain. The Anacostia has been one of the most polluted urban rivers in America — but it's slowly recovering.
🎯 Triage Questions
  1. What is the water cycle, and how do living organisms affect it?
  2. How does pollution in one place travel to harm people far away?
  3. What's one place in the world facing a major water crisis right now?
📖 Vocabulary You'll Encounter
water cycle evaporation condensation precipitation transpiration runoff groundwater watershed WASH water security
Crisis 03 · Linked to Concept 3 — Population Ecology

Populations on the Brink

03
🐘
🦏
Career Lens
Conservation Biologist · Wildlife Veterinarian
⚠️ Crisis Brief

African elephant populations have dropped more than 60% in 50 years. Monarch butterfly populations are crashing. Atlantic cod fisheries collapsed. Meanwhile, invasive species — English ivy in Rock Creek Park, lionfish in the Caribbean, zebra mussels in the Great Lakes — are erasing native populations at unprecedented speed.

You're a conservation biologist or wildlife vet. Your job: count populations, identify the limiting factors driving them down, and recommend interventions. If you don't act, species disappear forever. But how do you decide which population to save first when resources are limited?

📍 DC Connection Rock Creek Park is a 1,754-acre urban forest in our city — and it's being slowly strangled by invasive English ivy and garlic mustard. National Park Service biologists do quadrat sampling in Rock Creek to track which populations are surviving and which are losing ground. You'll do this same work on Day 9 of this unit.
🎯 Triage Questions
  1. What is "carrying capacity" and what makes a population grow or shrink?
  2. How do conservation biologists actually count wild populations?
  3. Name one species you'd advocate to save and why.
📖 Vocabulary You'll Encounter
population density carrying capacity limiting factor exponential growth logistic growth survivorship curve invasive species biodiversity extinction
Crisis 04 · Linked to Concept 6 — Relationships

The Pollution Pipeline

04
⚖️
🌍
Career Lens
Environmental Justice Advocate · Public Health Researcher
⚠️ Crisis Brief

Pollution doesn't fall equally. In country after country, in city after city, the people who breathe the dirtiest air, drink the dirtiest water, and live closest to landfills are almost always people of color and low-income communities. A factory that profits investors in one country may cause asthma rates to triple in a community 8,000 miles away.

You're an environmental justice advocate or public health researcher. You see ecosystems through the lens of relationships — between species, but also between human communities. Who benefits? Who pays the cost? Who has a voice, and who doesn't? Your job is to make the invisible visible.

📍 DC Connection Asthma rates in DC's Wards 7 and 8 are roughly twice as high as in Wards 2 and 3 — even though we all breathe the same regional air. Food deserts in our city sit miles from grocery stores. The Anacostia River runs through historically Black neighborhoods and was used as a dumping ground for a century. These aren't accidents — they're patterns of relationship.
🎯 Triage Questions
  1. What is environmental justice, and why does it matter?
  2. How do relationships between species (predator-prey, symbiosis) mirror relationships between human communities?
  3. Name one example, locally or globally, of pollution affecting one group more than another.
📖 Vocabulary You'll Encounter
predation competition mutualism commensalism parasitism symbiosis environmental justice public health ecosystem services food desert
Phase 3 · Choose Your Lens

Commit to a Career — for 12 Days

After rotating through all four stations, you'll choose ONE career lens. You'll carry it through every lesson in this unit. By Day 13, you'll deliver a professional briefing — your Capstone — from that perspective.

📓 Set Up Your Career Field Journal Today

  1. Open a new Google Doc titled "[Your Name] — Unit 7 Career Field Journal"
  2. Write your chosen career lens at the top in big letters
  3. Answer: "Why did I choose this lens? What about it spoke to me?" (3-5 sentences)
  4. Answer: "What do I already know about this work, and what do I want to learn?"
  5. Save and submit the link to Toddle by end of class — this is your through-line for 12 days
🏆 Day 13 · Your Capstone

The Career Briefing — Your Final Product

On Day 13, you'll deliver a professional briefing in the role of your adopted career, addressing a real ecological problem from your lens. You'll build it across the unit — every lesson contributes a section. You will not start from a blank page.

Section 1 · Concept 1Who is affected? Define the population, ecosystem, and key species at the heart of your case.
Section 2 · Concept 2How is water connecting this issue across borders? Trace the path from local to global.
Section 3 · Concept 3What do the population numbers say? Use real data, growth curves, and limiting factors.
Section 4 · Concept 6What relationships are driving this — between species, communities, and people?

Plus a Criterion D reflection: "As [your career], what is my responsibility? What ripple effects do my choices have?"

Quick Reference

🧠 ATL Skills You're Practicing Today

📌 MYP Command Terms for Unit 7

  • Identify — recognize and name (without full explanation)
  • Explain — give reasons with detail and mechanism
  • Analyze — break down and examine relationships
  • Evaluate — make a judgment with supporting evidence (Crit. D)
